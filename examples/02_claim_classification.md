# Example 02: a strict output contract, and accuracy you can quote

Script: [`02_claim_classification.py`](02_claim_classification.py) · Practice area: GI / P&C · Run: `python examples/02_claim_classification.py`

## The task, in actuarial terms

You have free-text claim notes (first notification, adjuster updates, the compressed shorthand people actually type into a claims system), and you want each one assigned to a reserving category. Done well this feeds triage, case-reserve setting, and the segmentation your development factors are built on. Done badly it corrupts the data on which every downstream reserving decision rests.

This is the shape of actuarial work most worth automating, and it is worth being precise about why. The output is a single label from a closed set, so it can be validated mechanically. The correct answer is knowable, so accuracy can be measured. The volume is high and the per-item judgement is shallow. Compare that with "draft the reserving commentary", where the output is unbounded prose, correctness is a matter of degree, and measurement is a research project. A local 8B model is a reasonable tool for the first task and a poor one for the second.

## The dataset

[`data/synthetic_claims.csv`](../data/synthetic_claims.csv) holds 200 fabricated notes, generated by committed code from templates and word lists under a fixed seed, so provenance is demonstrable and the set can be regenerated or extended. Notes run 22 to 34 words, median 26: realistically terse.

The five labels are exactly balanced at 40 notes each. That is convenient for reading a confusion matrix and unrepresentative of any real book, where motor damage-only claims will dwarf liability injury. Two consequences follow. Accuracy on a balanced set is not the accuracy you will see in production. And a real deployment needs to know its base rates, because a classifier that is 95 per cent accurate on a balanced sample can be worse than useless on a book where one class is 2 per cent of volume.

Roughly a fifth of the notes are deliberately hard, and the hard cases are the interesting part:

| Hard case | Why it is hard |
|---|---|
| Injury emerging after a damage-led note | The note opens describing vehicle damage and mentions a headache at the end |
| Flood water inside a motor claim | The word "water" points at `PROPERTY_WATER`, the incident is motor |
| Sprinkler water damage inside a fire loss | Same trap, opposite direction |
| Smoke-only damage, no flame | Is it fire? The rules say yes |
| Property repair costs inside a liability claim | Damage language inside an injury claim |

Every one of these is a case where surface vocabulary points one way and the labelling rule points another. That is not artificial difficulty; it is what claim notes are like.

## The rule that matters most: define labels once, use them three times

The label definitions appear in three places: the system prompt at [`02_claim_classification.py:29-44`](02_claim_classification.py#L29-L44), the labelling-rules table in [`data/README.md`](../data/README.md), and the versioned prompt record in [`prompts/claim_classifier_v1.md`](../prompts/claim_classifier_v1.md). They must say the same thing in all three.

This sounds like documentation hygiene. It is actually the foundation of whether your accuracy figure means anything. If the prompt says injury outranks damage and the gold labels were assigned on some other basis, then the number you report is measuring the disagreement between two definitions rather than the model's performance, and no amount of prompt engineering will fix it. A classification task is only as defensible as its label definitions, and the commonest failure in practice is not a weak model but an ambiguous rulebook.

The two tie-break rules exist precisely because the hard cases exist:

- Injury outranks damage. Any injury, however minor, whenever reported, makes a motor claim `MOTOR_BI`. This is a reserving decision expressed as a labelling rule: bodily injury claims develop differently and reserve differently, so misclassifying one as damage-only understates the tail.
- Origin governs. Water used to extinguish a fire is a fire loss. Again a reserving position, not a linguistic one.

Write the tie-breaks down, or the model will invent its own and apply them inconsistently.

## The output contract, and why the parser distrusts it

The prompt ends: *"Respond with the category code only. No punctuation, no explanation."* That is the contract. The code does not trust it:

```python
def parse_label(raw: str) -> str | None:
    cleaned = strip_think(raw).upper()
    found = [lb for lb in LABELS if lb in cleaned]
    if len(found) == 1 and len(cleaned) < 40:
        return found[0]
    return None
```

Three deliberate decisions in five lines.

`len(found) == 1` rejects any response mentioning two labels. A reply of "MOTOR_PD, though possibly MOTOR_BI" is not a classification, it is a hedge, and accepting the first match would silently convert a hedge into a decision.

`len(cleaned) < 40` rejects a correct label buried in an explanation. This looks pedantic and is not: a model that has started explaining has stopped obeying the contract, and the run where it explains is the run where it also reorders or qualifies. Enforcing the contract's shape, not just its content, is what keeps the parse honest.

Returning `None` rather than guessing is the important one. As the docstring puts it: an invalid response is *information*. It is a formatting failure you count, log, and in production route to a human, never silently repair. The moment you add a fuzzy fallback that recovers a label from a malformed reply, your invalid-output rate goes to zero and you lose your only cheap signal that the model's behaviour has drifted.

## The measurement

Three numbers, and they answer different questions:

| Metric | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | Of the notes it classified, how many matched the gold label? |
| Invalid rate | How often did it fail to produce a parseable answer at all? |
| Confusion pairs | *Which* mistakes, in which direction? |

Keeping accuracy and invalid rate separate is a design decision worth defending. A model that is 90 per cent accurate with a 0 per cent invalid rate and a model that is 90 per cent accurate with an 8 per cent invalid rate are not the same system. The second is telling you something is wrong (a prompt that no longer fits, a model update, a note format it has not seen), and a single blended score would hide it.

The confusion pairs are where the actuarial reading happens. Direction matters more than count. `MOTOR_BI → MOTOR_PD` means injury claims are being booked as damage-only, which understates the tail and is a reserving problem. `MOTOR_PD → MOTOR_BI` inflates BI counts, which is wrong but far less dangerous. Symmetric error counts can carry very asymmetric financial consequence, and this is exactly the judgement a reserving actuary is equipped to make and a data scientist often is not.

## Running it

```bash
python examples/02_claim_classification.py            # 25-note sample, the default
python examples/02_claim_classification.py --n 50     # larger sample
python examples/02_claim_classification.py --all      # all 200
python examples/02_claim_classification.py --dry-run  # first three prompts, no server
```

The 25-note default exists so the example finishes in a minute or two. Treat it as a smoke test, not a measurement: on 25 notes with balanced classes, a single misclassification moves accuracy by four percentage points, and the confidence interval around any figure you quote is embarrassingly wide. Use `--all` before you believe a number, and note that this is one sequential call per note, so 200 notes takes a while on an 8 GB card.

`--dry-run` needs no server at all: the `openai` import sits inside `main()` for exactly that reason. Reading the three prompts it prints is the fastest way to see what the model is actually being asked.

Row-level output lands in `results/claim_classification_results.csv` with the gold label and the prediction side by side. That file is the artefact worth keeping: an aggregate accuracy figure is a claim, and the row-level results are the evidence for it.

## What this example does not do

Being explicit about the gaps is more useful than implying completeness.

No confidence or abstention. The model returns a label, not a probability. A production classifier should be able to say "I am not sure, send this to a human", and the cheapest approximation is to treat the invalid-output path as an abstention channel rather than an error.

No human baseline. Accuracy is quoted against gold labels as though those were truth. In real claims work two experienced handlers disagree on a meaningful fraction of notes, and a model at 88 per cent against a gold standard that humans reproduce at 91 per cent is a very different result from the same model measured against a standard humans reproduce at 99 per cent. Establishing inter-rater agreement on a sample is unglamorous and changes how you read every subsequent number.

No drift monitoring. One run, one number. Real deployment needs the same evaluation on a schedule, because note-writing conventions change, claims systems get replaced, and model updates land underneath you.

Synthetic data, and its distribution. These notes came from templates. Real notes have inconsistent abbreviation, misspellings, other languages, pasted email chains, and structure that varies by handler and by office. Expect measured accuracy to fall on real data, and expect the invalid-output rate to rise.

## The governance reading

In US actuarial terms this is a model, and ASOP 56 expectations attach: understand what it does, test it, document its limitations, and be explicit about reliance on a model developed by others. The three artefacts this example naturally produces map onto those expectations: the versioned prompt is the specification, the results CSV is the test evidence, and [`governance/model_card_template.md`](../governance/model_card_template.md) is where the intended use, out-of-scope uses, and known failure modes get recorded.

Against the NIST AI RMF, this example is where MEASURE lives. An accuracy figure against a known-correct sample, tracked over time, is the evidence that framework asks for and the artefact most actuarial teams have never built. ## Things to try

- Run `--all` on `qwen3:8b`, then again with `LLM_MODEL=qwen3:4b`. The accuracy gap between a 4B and 8B model on a well-specified task with a tight contract is usually smaller than people expect, and that gap is the entire economic argument for small local models.
- Remove "Injury outranks damage" from the system prompt and re-run. Watch `MOTOR_BI → MOTOR_PD` appear in the confusions. This is the most direct demonstration available that tie-break rules are load-bearing.
- Open the results CSV and read the notes the model got wrong. In most runs a few of them are cases where you will find yourself agreeing with the model and doubting the gold label, which is a finding about your rulebook, not the model.
- Compare against a keyword baseline: assign `MOTOR_BI` if the note contains "injur", and so on. Knowing what a crude rule achieves is the only way to judge whether the model is earning its place.
